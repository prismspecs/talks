# Digital Sabotage — Speaker Notes

*Expanded from the Pioneer Works talk ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GjKb4h4lSbs)) with additional reference material for the full deck.*

---

## Post-Awareness Art {#post-awareness}

> "We are at peak awareness. Everyone more or less knows what's going on. The real challenge is how to move beyond that and intervene directly on the systems themselves rather than produce more evidence of why the system is so messed up."

Society has reached peak awareness — producing evidence of broken systems is no longer sufficient. The goal is to move beyond documentation and awareness toward direct intervention on the systems that produce injustice, deliberately blurring the line between conceptual art and functional sabotage.

---

## The Monkey Wrench Gang — Edward Abbey (1975) {#monkey-wrench-gang}

The origin of the term **"monkeywrenching."** Abbey's novel popularized sabotage as environmental defense — literally throwing a wrench into the gears of industry to halt ecological destruction. It frames physical sabotage as a necessary, moral response to systemic harm, setting the philosophical foundation for translating these tactics into the digital sphere.

---

## FloodNet — Ricardo Dominguez / Electronic Disturbance Theater (1998) {#floodnet}

The birth of the **virtual sit-in**, and of legal framing as artistic strategy. A downloadable program that made a user's computer part of a DDoS attack, sending millions of low-level pings. Used against the Mexican government and the World Economic Forum.

**Legal precedent:** Dominguez successfully argued in court that FloodNet was protected as free speech. By framing it as a "virtual sit-in," it was granted historical continuity with the civil rights sit-ins of the 1960s — using artistic and historical framing as a legal shield.

---

## eToy War / ®TMark (1999) {#etoy-war}

The gamification of dissent, and corporate sabotage. eToys.com launched a cease-and-desist against the European net-art group eToy over domain/trademark infringement. ®TMark and activists responded with a gamified sabotage campaign: participants earned points for escalating media coverage — 10 points for a negative online review, 100 for local news, 10,000 for national coverage. The resulting PR crisis contributed to the corporation's stock price crashing.

---

## Tripwire — Tad Hirsch (2006) {#tripwire}

Automated bureaucratic friction. Produced with the Institute for Applied Autonomy for the 2006 01SJ Biennial: custom-built noise sensors, hidden in coconuts, hung in a public park near the San Jose airport. Excessive airplane noise triggered automated telephone calls to the airport's complaint line — on behalf of residents and wildlife.

---

## Contestational Robotics — Institute for Applied Autonomy (2003) {#contestational-robotics}

Subverting automation for protest. Projects like *Graffiti Writer* — a teleoperated robot that spray-paints messages on the street — demonstrated how technology typically reserved for state surveillance or industrial capital could be repurposed for civilian dissent and autonomous action.

---

## SETI@home {#seti}

The power of distributed computing and active participation. Launched in 1999 because Berkeley couldn't afford supercomputer time to analyze satellite data from the Puerto Rico array. Distributing raw data to volunteers created an ad hoc distributed supercomputer.

> "It's not really like volunteering or donating. It's another step. You are doing the thing itself as well with them. It transforms you into a more invested participant."

It proved that collective goals could be achieved using idle computing power — shifting the paradigm from passive charity/volunteering to direct material participation. Users are *doing the thing itself*.

---

## Bail Bloc {#bail-bloc}

Distributed participation turned toward abolitionist sabotage. A background desktop app, inspired by SETI@home, that uses idle processing power — roughly the resources of a Netflix stream — to mine Monero. The crypto is converted to USD to subvert the carceral state's financial leverage.

Partnered with the Bronx Freedom Fund and the Connecticut Immigrant Bond Fund to release people, including those detained by ICE.

---

## Tax Deductible Expenses (Grayson Earle, 2016) {#tax-deductible}

Weaponizing bureaucratic loopholes. A performance piece: every mundane purchase made over a year — food, clothing — was recorded and deemed "art materials" for a performance about consuming the item. This allowed the expenses to be written off as professional tax deductions — a critique of corporate tax dodging, and a prefigurative tactic for everyday resistance.

---

## Hacking Hans Haacke at the New Museum (Grayson Earle, 2020) {#haacke-hack}

Institutional critique via data manipulation. An intervention hacking the iPads used for the Hans Haacke retrospective survey at the New Museum: hundreds of thousands of falsified entries were injected to protest the museum's union-busting efforts — highlighting the hypocrisy of an institution displaying leftist critique while suppressing workers' rights.

---

## Hans Haacke's MoMA Poll (1970) {#haacke-moma-poll}

The analog predecessor to data-driven institutional critique. For the 1970 *Information* exhibition, Haacke asked museum-goers to vote on Governor Rockefeller's failure to denounce Nixon's Indochina policy. Since Rockefeller was a MoMA board member, the artwork weaponized audience participation against the institution's own leadership.

---

## Slow Hot Computer — Sam Lavigne (2015) {#slow-hot-computer}

Speculative workplace slacking. A website with a single function: make the user's computer run slow and hot by intentionally exhausting system resources. A tool for digital time theft — giving workers an excuse for lowered productivity.

---

## Synthetic Messenger — Tega Brain & Sam Lavigne (2021) {#synthetic-messenger}

Algorithmic disruption and financial sabotage. A botnet that artificially clicks on climate-change advertisements to inflate their algorithmic value and drain the ad budgets of fossil-fuel companies — intervening directly in the attention economy.

---

## Sabotage — a brief etymology {#sabotage-etymology}

From *sabot* — the wooden clog of the French worker. The story goes that workers threw their clogs into factory machinery to jam the gears and stop production. Historians dispute whether clogs were ever really thrown, but the word stuck: stopping the machine from the inside.

*Redneck*, too, is said to come from a worker's garment — the red bandana coal miners wore as a sign of solidarity in the twentieth century.

---

## 79% Work Clock — Disobedient Electronics, PARTY {#work-clock}

Physicalizing systemic inequity. Created for Equal Pay Day, this physical alarm clock alerts women when 79% of the workday has passed, reflecting the wage gap. It proposes a direct action: if compensation is only 79%, labor output should cease at exactly that threshold.

---

## Transparency Grenade — Julian Oliver (2012) {#transparency-grenade}

Forced transparency. A device shaped like a hand grenade that captures network traffic and audio from the surrounding environment, securely leaking it to a public server — a tool for corporate or governmental whistleblowing.

---

## Reflecto Cube / Protest Cube — Colectivo Enmedio / Tools for Action {#reflecto-cube}

Defensive tactical architecture. DIY giant silver inflatable cubes used in demonstrations to halt police riot charges — aesthetically beautiful and highly practical. They physically block police lines, buffer against batons, and reflect police lights/flashes back at authorities to cause momentary vision loss. Re-inflatable and reusable.

---

## Kellogg's Hack — Sean Black (@black_madness21) (2021) {#kelloggs-hack}

Algorithmic solidarity and strike defense. During the 2021 Kellogg's strike, a TikTok user wrote a script automating the creation of fake resumes, flooding the company's job portal — sabotaging Kellogg's attempt to hire scab labor to replace striking union workers.

---

## Workshop: Speculative Tools for Workplace Protest {#workshop}

Developing grassroots sabotage tailored to specific labor issues — wage transparency, inability to unionize. Participants gather (in person or Zoom) to discuss workplace problems and invent tools to address them; some projects are speculative, some are very real.

**Favorite idea — the Printer Hack:** hacking office printers so that before any legitimate print job, a sheet of union organizing material prints first, or forcing the printer to output solid black pages to burn through the company's ink budget as a form of financial friction.

---

## Themes {#themes}

- **Post-awareness:** stop documenting injustice; start intervening on the systems that produce it.
- **Distributed participation:** not charity or volunteering — contributing computing power to *do the thing itself* (DDoS, crypto mining, data crunching).
- **Legal framing as artistic strategy:** the "virtual sit-in" precedent protects digital protest by grounding it in civil rights lineage.
- **Gamification of dissent:** eToy's point system turns spectatorship into collective action.
- **Speculative tools with real teeth:** from printer hacks to crypto-for-bail pipelines — the line between concept and function is deliberately blurred.
